The shell and SSH

The terminal

The terminal is a command line inside EiN's web interface: a shell that works on the same tree of nodes that the pages show, with the same commands and the same permissions. This page is for anyone who signs in and wants to use it: how to open it, work in tabs, move around with the keyboard, find earlier commands, get help, and find the command you need.

Open the terminal

  1. Sign in. Visitors browsing without an account have no terminal.
  2. Choose the terminal button in the top bar (tooltip Toggle Terminal (Alt+T)), or press Alt+T wherever you are.

The terminal drops down from the top of the window over the page, half the window high, with the cursor ready at the prompt. Try a first command:

# in EiN's terminal
ls /archive
albums  devices  locations  people  tags  tracks

That is an EiN with the Archive installed, which makes every folder there but tags. Without it, /archive holds only tags, and ls / shows the rest of the tree.

To hide it again, press Alt+T, choose the chevron at the right end of the tab bar (Hide terminal (Alt+T)), or click anywhere outside it. Hiding the terminal does not stop it: the shells keep running, a command you started goes on, and the terminal is as you left it when you open it again.

On a phone-sized screen the top bar has no terminal button; Alt+T still works with a keyboard attached. From your own computer you can also reach the same shell over SSH (see Use the same shell over SSH).

Administrators have one more way in: in the Nodes explorer, right-click a folder and choose Open terminal here. EiN opens a new tab and types cd to that folder for you.

Read the prompt

The prompt says who you are and where you are:

helen@:~$
  • helen is your username, in green, or in red for an administrator.
  • Between @ and : stands the host name, which is empty unless you set one, for example with export HOSTNAME=ein in your ~/.bashrc.
  • ~ is the folder you are in. ~ stands for your home folder, /home/helen; elsewhere you see the path, such as /archive/albums/christmas-2019.
  • $ becomes # when the shell acts as an administrator.

Folders and files are nodes of EiN's tree: paths start at /, .. is the folder above, and cd moves you around. The tree shows what lies where.

Work in several tabs

The tab bar runs along the foot of the terminal. Each tab is a shell of its own, with its own current folder, its own variables and whatever is running in it; switching tabs does not stop the others.

  • Add a tab: choose + (New session (Alt+N)) or press Alt+N. The first tab is called Session 1, the next Session 2, and so on. If the terminal is hidden, Alt+N adds the tab without showing it.
  • Know which is which: once a tab's shell has reported its folder, the tab shows that path instead of its name, such as /home/helen or /archive/albums/christmas-2019.
  • Switch: click a tab, or press Alt+← and Alt+→.
  • Close a tab: choose its ×, or press Alt+W while you are typing in it. Closing a tab ends its shell and everything running in it. The last tab has no × and cannot be closed.
  • End a shell from inside: type exit, or press Ctrl+D on an empty line. The tab goes away; if it was the only one, a fresh shell takes its place.

Make the terminal taller or shorter

The terminal starts at half the window's height.

  • Maximize (Alt+M), the button at the right of the tab bar, makes it fill the window. Pressed again, as Restore (Alt+M), it returns to half the window.
  • Alt+↓ makes it a tenth of the window taller, Alt+↑ a tenth shorter, between a tenth and the whole window.

The browser remembers the height for the next time, on that computer and in that browser.

The terminal's own keys

The ? button at the right of the tab bar (Terminal shortcuts) lists the keys of the terminal itself. They work whether you are typing in the terminal or not, and only with Alt alone, without Ctrl, Shift or ⌘:

Keys In the ? list What happens
Alt+T Toggle terminal Shows or hides the terminal
Alt+N New tab Adds a tab with a new shell
Alt+W Close tab Closes the tab you are typing in; never the last one
Alt+M Maximize Fills the window, or returns to half the window
Alt+← Previous tab Moves to the tab on the left, from the first to the last
Alt+→ Next tab Moves to the tab on the right, from the last to the first
Alt+↑ Shrink height A tenth of the window shorter
Alt+↓ Grow height A tenth of the window taller

The Keyboard shortcuts dialog that ? opens anywhere in EiN lists only the first two, under Anywhere. Keyboard shortcuts has every key in EiN.

Note

On a Mac, ⌥ with a letter types a character of its own (⌥T types †), while the terminal looks for the letter itself, so ⌥T, ⌥N, ⌥W and ⌥M may not reach it. The top-bar button and the buttons in the tab bar do the same. ⌥ with the arrow keys is not affected.

Come back to a running shell

Reload the page, or lose the connection for a moment, and the terminal comes back as you left it: the same tabs, open or hidden as before, and in each tab the same shell, in the same folder, with up to 1000 lines of what scrolled past drawn again. A command that was running carries on while the page is away.

  • How long EiN waits. EiN keeps a shell without a page for five minutes. The administrator sets this with Terminal reconnect grace (CFG_TERMINAL_RECONNECT_GRACE_SEC, 300 seconds; 0 ends a shell as soon as its page goes; a change applies from the next disconnect). After that the shell ends, with whatever runs in it, and the tab starts a fresh one when you come back. To keep a long job going after you leave, see The shell language.
  • One browser tab, one set of terminals. The tabs belong to the browser tab they were opened in. EiN in a second browser tab gets terminals of its own, starting again at Session 1, and closing a browser tab leaves its shells to end after the five minutes.
  • Signing out ends every terminal of that sign-in and starts the tabs over, so the next person who signs in on that browser finds none of your shells.
  • When EiN restarts, for example for an update, every shell ends. The tabs stay, and each starts a fresh shell once EiN answers again.
  • While the connection is down, the top bar shows a red Disconnected badge, and each tab whose shell is not connected shows a red crossed-out plug in place of its terminal icon. The terminal reconnects by itself; there is nothing to press.

Your command history is kept through all of this (see Find an earlier command).

Upload files by dropping them

Drag files from your computer onto the terminal and let go. EiN uploads them into the folder the tab is in, the path the tab shows. The Uploads button in the top bar follows them, as for any upload, and a file whose name is taken in that folder is stored under a numbered name beside it.

Where you drop decides what happens next. With the Archive installed, go to a staging category or an album first to have it take new pictures in:

# in EiN's terminal
cd ~/staging/files/inbox

Then drop the files. Getting your photos in explains staging, albums and what happens after an upload. You need permission to write in the folder; an upload into a folder you may only read fails and is listed under Uploads.

Show a picture with cat

cat on a picture or a video draws it right in the terminal, 20 lines tall, wherever the file is in the tree. Here it draws a photo in one of the Archive's albums:

# in EiN's terminal
cd /archive/albums/christmas-2019 && cat 20191225_155434.jpg
  • cat -s draws it half as tall, cat -l twice as tall.
  • export MEDIA_LINES=30 changes the height for the rest of the session (see Environment variables).
  • A video plays at once, with its controls under it. A recording gets a player two lines tall and starts playing.
  • A text file is printed with colours for its language, such as JSON, YAML, bash or Markdown.

Only the terminal in the browser draws pictures. Over SSH, cat prints marker lines starting __IMG: instead.

Note

A picture whose path holds a space or a colon, such as one in /archive/albums/Summer 2020, is not drawn: the terminal shows its __IMG: lines instead. Open it in the web interface.

Edit the command line

The line you type is edited with the keys of bash on Linux, in its default (emacs) mode. The ones used most:

Keys What they do
Tab Complete what you are typing (see Let Tab complete)
↑ ↓ The previous and the next command from your history
Ctrl+R Search your history as you type
Home End, or Ctrl+A Ctrl+E Start and end of the line
Ctrl+← Ctrl+→ A word left or right
Ctrl+K / Ctrl+U Cut from the cursor to the end / to the start of the line
Ctrl+Y Paste what you cut last
Alt+. Insert the last word of the previous command (not with ⌥ on a Mac)
Ctrl+C At the prompt: drop the line (^C is printed). While a command runs: stop it
Ctrl+D On an empty line: end the shell and its tab
Ctrl+L Clear the screen, as the command clear does

Some keys mean something else in the browser:

  • Alt+←, Alt+→, Alt+↑ and Alt+↓ are the terminal's own keys for tabs and height; move by word with Ctrl+← and Ctrl+→.
  • Ctrl+R searches your history instead of reloading the page. To reload, press F5.
  • The browser keeps some keys for itself before the terminal sees them. In most browsers Ctrl+W closes the browser tab rather than cutting a word; use Alt+W to close a terminal tab.

Ctrl+Z does not pause a running command. Start a long one in the background with & instead; The shell language covers jobs.

Find an earlier command

Press ↑ to step back through the commands you ran, ↓ to step forward again.

To find one by a word in it, press Ctrl+R and type part of it. The prompt turns into the search and shows the newest command that matches:

(reverse-i-search)`peg': person-info /archive/people/Peggy_Wrenfield
  • Ctrl+R again finds the next older match.
  • Enter runs the command found.
  • Esc leaves it on the line for you to change first.
  • Ctrl+G gives up and brings back what you had typed.

bash's history commands work too: history lists what you ran, !! runs the last command again, !42 runs number 42 of the list, and ^old^new runs the last command with old replaced by new.

What EiN keeps:

  • Your history is the file ~/.bash_history. Every terminal tab and every SSH login of yours adds to it as each command runs, so a tab you open afterwards finds the commands typed in the others. A tab that is already open keeps the list it started with.
  • A command typed with a space in front, or the same as the one before it, is not kept.
  • It keeps the last 1000 commands. Change this under Profile → Preferences, Shell history length (HISTSIZE); a tab you open afterwards uses the new length. Your profile, favourites and trash shows where.
  • Commands the web pages run for you, and commands sent with ssh host 'command', are not kept.

Let Tab complete

Press Tab while you type a command name, a path, an option, a user or a group. When only one thing fits, EiN fills it in, with / after a folder and a space after anything else, and quotes it if it holds spaces. When several fit, it fills in as much as they share; press Tab again to list them all.

The first two rows are on an EiN with the Archive installed:

You type Tab gives
cd /archive/al cd /archive/albums/
person-info /archive/people/Peg person-info /archive/people/Peggy_Wrenfield
search --ki search --kind=, ready for the value
user-info he user-info helen
pipeline, a space, Tab twice the list of pipeline's subcommands

Completion reads each command's own description of its arguments and options, so it knows a command's options without being told. A command of a family, such as pipeline status, is completed as two words. Scripts of your own can take part; see The shell language.

Get help on a command

Put --help (or -h) straight after a command's name. The command does not run; it says how to use it instead:

# in EiN's terminal
onthisday --help
Usage: onthisday [OPTIONS] <day>

What the archive has from a calendar day (MM-DD, default today) in earlier years, from every onthisday-* command on PATH

Arguments:
  <day>    day as MM-DD (default today, UTC)

Options:
  --limit <number>  entries returned across all providers (default 8)
  -h, --help        Show this help message

Returns:
  the entries of every onthisday-* provider, newest year first: the year, what, the node it is about, which provider, and the route that draws it.

Examples:
  onthisday
  onthisday 06-21 --limit 20
  • A family of commands, such as config, docs, group, mod, pipeline, pool, service and user, lists its members with --help: service --help. Each member answers for itself: service status --help. The two words are another way of typing the member's name: service status runs service-status.
  • bash's own commands, such as cd, export and history, are described by help: help cd.
  • -h first always means help, even for a command whose own -h means something else: du -h shows the help of du. Put another option first, as in du -sh.

There is no man and no pager. Long output scrolls past; scroll back with the mouse wheel or the scrollbar.

The same descriptions are in Documentation in the sidebar, under Commands, for every command installed on your EiN, and on this site in the command reference.

Find the command you need

When you know what you want done but not which command does it, ask by what it does.

In the finder. Press Ctrl+K (⌘+K on a Mac) and type > followed by what the command should do, or part of its name. Up to six commands appear under Commands; choosing one opens its description in Documentation. It does not run the command: you run it in the terminal. Search and the finder has the details.

In the terminal. docs find lists the best commands, best first, each with a score, its name and its description:

# in EiN's terminal
docs find restart a service

With the assistant module installed there is also assistant-commands find, which adds whether each command only reads or also changes something, and assistant-commands judge tells you which a whole command line would do:

# in EiN's terminal
assistant-commands find 'list running services'
assistant-commands judge 'service restart pipeline-pruner'

How well this works depends on what is installed:

Installed How commands are found
Core alone Your words are matched against the commands' names and descriptions; a word in the name counts double
The assistant module The assistant answers for docs find and the finder, and still matches words
The assistant module and the media service Commands are found by meaning, so one can turn up even when its description uses other words than yours

The settings behind this are Command finder (CFG_COMMAND_FINDER) and Text embedder (CFG_TEXT_EMBEDDER); the assistant module and the media service set them when they are installed.

Use the same shell over SSH

The terminal is one of several interfaces to the same shell. From your own computer you can log in over SSH with your EiN username and password, on port 2222 unless your administrator chose another:

# on your own computer
ssh -p 2222 helen@ein.local

You get the same shell as in a terminal tab: the same prompt, your history, your ~/.bashrc, Tab completion and the same keys. Two things belong to the browser alone: pictures and videos drawn by cat, and dropping files to upload them. Over SSH, copy files with SFTP or scp instead.

The web pages run these same commands for you, and so does the assistant, always as the person signed in. A command you may not run in the terminal is refused through every other interface too. SSH and SFTP covers keys, running one command at a time, and copying files.

Commands worth knowing

Commands marked archive come with the Archive and exist only where it is installed, as do the /archive/albums and /archive/people paths in the examples. search, news, events and onthisday are EiN's own, but answer with what the installed modules provide, so without a module they find nothing. Commands by task has many more, grouped by what you want to do.

Command What it does
ls Lists what is in a folder; ls -l with details
cd Moves into a folder; cd alone goes home, cd - back to where you were
cat Prints a text file, or draws a picture
edit Edits a text file in the terminal; see The editor
cp Copies; cp -r for a folder and what is in it
mv Moves or renames, and keeps the links to what it moves pointing at the new place
rm Deletes for good; rm -r for anything that has children, a processed photo included
trash trash put <path> moves into your trash, from which you can restore it
mkdir Makes a folder; mkdir -p makes the folders above it too
mkfile Makes an empty text file, what touch does elsewhere
du Adds up sizes; du -chs * gives readable sizes and a total
find Finds nodes under a path, as GNU find does: find /archive/people -maxdepth 1 -type person
search Searches what the modules make searchable: search christmas --kind photo
stat Shows a node's details: type, owner, times
ln ln -s makes a softlink, one thing in a second place
news What has been added lately, newest first
events What falls on the days around today, such as birthdays and anniversaries
onthisday What the modules have from this day in earlier years
timeline archive: albums, life events, birthdays and tracks, newest first
person-info archive: a person's details, relations, homes and events
top Watches the system live; q quits
ps, who What is running now, and who is connected; your own, or everyone's for an administrator
health One line on how the system is doing, and the checks behind it
docs docs find <words> finds a command by what it does
whoami Prints your username

Warning

rm deletes at once and for good: it does not use the trash, and nothing brings the node back. Use trash put for anything you may want again; it then shows under Trash in your account menu (see Your profile, favourites and trash).

top fills the terminal with what EiN is doing and keeps it up to date. Press Alt+M first to give it the whole window. Health and the dashboard explains what it shows and all its keys.

Steps that belong together can be joined with &&, and paths with spaces go in quotes, as in bash:

# in EiN's terminal
cd "/archive/albums/Summer 2020" && ls -l

The shell language covers what else bash syntax can do in EiN, and how it differs from bash on Linux.

If something goes differently

There is no terminal button. You are not signed in, or the window is phone-sized. Sign in; on a small screen press Alt+T with a keyboard, or use SSH from a computer.

The terminal closed while I was clicking on the page. A click outside the terminal hides it. Its shells keep running; press Alt+T to bring it back.

The top bar says Disconnected, or a tab shows a red crossed-out plug. EiN cannot be reached from the browser just now: the badge is the page's own connection, the plug that tab's shell. The terminal reconnects by itself and finds its shell again if the connection is back within five minutes.

The screen was cleared and a fresh prompt waits. The shell ended: you were away longer than five minutes, EiN restarted, or you signed out. Your history is still there; ↑ and Ctrl+R find your commands.

error: command 'albm' not found. No command of that name is on your path. Check the spelling with Tab, or look for it with docs find. Commands such as timeline and person-info exist only where their module is installed.

service-restart: permission denied: only an administrator may run it. The command is for administrators. Through every interface it is refused the same way; ask your administrator.

du -h shows help instead of sizes. -h straight after a command's name always asks for help. Write du -sh or du -ch.

cat prints lines starting __IMG: instead of the picture. You are connected over SSH, or the picture's path holds a space or a colon. Open the picture in the web interface.

Dropped files landed in the wrong folder. They go into the folder the tab is in, the path on the tab. cd to where they belong and drop them again, or move them with mv.

The browser tab closed when I pressed Ctrl+W. The browser keeps that key for itself. Open EiN again: you get new terminals, and the old shells end after five minutes.
