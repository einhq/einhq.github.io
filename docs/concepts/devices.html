Concepts

Devices, endpoints and drivers

A device is something outside EiN that reports or can be told what to do: a temperature sensor, a plug, a button, a phone that says where it is, a GPS logger whose files you copy in. EiN keeps each one in the tree at /dev/<device>, with what it reports and what it can be told as files under it. This page explains how that is laid out, how a device's readings get in and go out, and what moves them. Read it before writing to devices from the terminal or a script, or before binding a queue to them. Devices covers the pages, and Devices and their keys who may do what.

A device is a folder

Devices sit directly under /dev, beside EiN's own null and event routers. A device is a node of the type device, a folder, and its endpoints are the nodes in it, much as Linux shows a device's values as files in a folder under /sys:

/dev/
├── events                  EiN's own: where changes are announced
├── null                    EiN's own: reads nothing, swallows what is written
├── hall-plug/              a device, found by a driver
│   ├── switch-0            a control: on or off
│   ├── switch-0-power      a log of readings, in W
│   └── switch-0-energy     a log of readings, in Wh
├── porch-button/
│   └── input-0             a log of events: single, double, long
└── ruby-phone/             a device made by hand, driven by core's tracker
    ├── .key                its device key
    └── position            a track of positions

A device's name is lower-case letters, digits and hyphens, made from what the device or a person calls it: Ruby phone becomes ruby-phone. The names null, events, pipeline-events, tty, stdin, stdout and stderr are EiN's own and are refused. The name is what commands, rules and routing keys use; the device's label is what the pages show, and can be changed without touching the name.

What a device says about itself is in its attributes:

Attribute What it holds
label What the pages call it
class What it is: phone, tracker, plug, switch, light, button, sensor, boat, …
driver The driver that reads and writes it; none for a device whose endpoints are written to
manufacturer Who made it
model Its model
firmware The firmware it runs, when it says
serial Its serial number
mac Its network address
online Whether it answers now, when its driver can tell
config What a person gave its driver when making it (device-create --config)

attrs /dev/hall-plug prints them, and device-show prints the device with each of its endpoints.

Endpoints

An endpoint is one thing a device reports or is told. There are three types:

Type What it is Reading it gives Writing it
log A series of values, or of events Each value with its time A value, kept as the next entry
track A series of positions Each position with its time A position, kept as the next one
control A value written to change the device, a switch The states the device reported, in order A value, checked and sent to the device through its driver

A log of events is a button's: each entry is a press, such as single, double or long, rather than a state, and the same press twice is two entries. Into any other log a driver puts a value only when it differs from the one before (see How often a reading is kept).

A control is not a store of what you wrote. Writing on to /dev/hall-plug/switch-0 sends it to the plug; what reading the control gives is what the plug then reported it was. Until it reports, the control remembers what was asked, who asked and when (its requested attribute), and the device's page shows the switch as asked, waiting for the device.

What a control takes is its schema:

Schema It takes Example
bool on or off (true, false, 1, 0, yes, no), or toggle, the opposite of now a relay, a lamp
number A number, within the control's least and greatest and in its steps a thermostat's 21.5
enum One of the control's options a heat pump's eco
text Any line a display's message
momentary press: something the device does once, with no state a restart, a chime

toggle needs a state the device has reported; before the first, it is refused. A value the schema does not take is refused with what it would take, such as hall-plug/switch-0 takes on, off or toggle, not '7', and nothing is sent.

An endpoint carries attributes of its own: its label, unit (°C, W), deviceClass (what is measured: temperature, power, door, motion), retention (how long its entries are kept, such as 365 day or forever), minInterval, and for a control its schema. An endpoint whose category is diagnostic or config tells of the device itself, such as its own temperature or its Wi-Fi signal, and the pages put it apart. One the device announced in a way its driver cannot read is marked supported: false and keeps nothing; one the device no longer announces is marked retired, and keeps its history.

Drivers

A driver is what turns a device's own language into endpoints and back: it reads what the device sends and appends it to the endpoints, and it sends the device what is written to its controls. A device names its driver in its driver attribute.

Driver Comes with What it drives
tracker EiN itself A phone, a GPS tracker or a logger: what it posts, or a GPX file written to it, becomes its position track
ha-discovery the Automation module Anything that announces itself over MQTT the way Home Assistant reads: ESPHome, Zigbee2MQTT and others
shelly the Automation module Shelly's second generation and later, over MQTT
shelly-gen1 the Automation module Shelly's first generation (Plug S, 1, 1PM, 2.5, i3), over MQTT

devices --drivers lists the drivers running now, with the module each comes from and how many devices it drives. A module's drivers run while its service does: when the service is stopped, a write to one of their devices is refused with <device>'s driver <driver> is not running, and the device's Info tab says (not running) beside the driver.

Most drivers find their devices: a sensor announces itself on the MQTT broker, and its driver makes the device and its endpoints within seconds. A phone or a GPS logger announces nothing, so a member of the devices group makes it by hand with device-create, naming its driver:

# in EiN's terminal
device-create 'Ruby phone' --driver tracker --class phone

The driver gives the new device what it has from the start, here an empty position track. A device made without a driver has no endpoints of its own and takes nothing written to the device itself.

Core's tracker driver

tracker reads everything a phone or a GPS tracker sends, and adds each position that has a time to the device's position track:

  • what Traccar Client posts, as JSON;
  • the OsmAnd protocol, a query such as ?lat=59.32&lon=18.07&timestamp=1794132000, sent by OsmAnd, older Traccar Client versions and many hardware trackers;
  • a GPRMC sentence, ?gprmc=$GPRMC,…;
  • a GPX file, as a logger or a tracking app saves it;
  • EiN's own positions, a line of JSON each: {"lat": 59.32, "lng": 18.07, "ts": "2026-11-08T09:00:00Z"}.

A position at a time the track already has is not added twice, so sending the same file again adds nothing.

Writing to a device

What is written to the device itself, /dev/<device>, is raw input for its driver: a GPX file, a tracker's post, whatever the driver reads. What is written to an endpoint is a value in EiN's own form. Both work the same from the terminal, over SFTP, and over HTTP:

# in EiN's terminal
cat ~/summer-walk.gpx > /dev/old-logger                  # raw input: the tracker driver reads the GPX
echo on > /dev/hall-plug/switch-0                        # a value for a control, sent to the plug
echo 41.5 > /dev/garden-sensor/soil-moisture             # a value appended to a log
cat /dev/hall-plug/switch-0                              # the states the plug reported
tail -f /dev/hall-plug/switch-0-power                    # each new reading, until Ctrl+C

A write to a device or a control is answered when its driver has taken it: echo fails at once when the value is refused or the driver is not running, and a control's write that the device has not taken within 10 seconds fails with <device> did not answer within 10 s.

A write to a log or a track endpoint appends to it, with > as with >>: an endpoint keeps its history, unlike a file or a log elsewhere in the tree, which > empties first. A value written to a log endpoint by hand, a number, true or false, or a word, is kept as {"value": …}, as a driver's readings are, so the device's page and device-history read it; a line that is a JSON object is kept as it is. Every log a driver makes has this from its valueField attribute, and a log made by hand under a device gets it with attrs -s valueField=value <log>. A value written by hand is neither throttled nor published on the bus: only what a driver appends is. A control takes >; >> on a control would append a state as if the device had reported it, which only the administrator may do.

Over HTTP the device's file in the tree takes the same writes at /file/dev/<device> and /file/dev/<device>/<endpoint>, where a body of a bare value, such as 21.4, is kept as {"value": 21.4}. A device cannot sign in, so it sends its device key with them, which opens its own folder for these writes and nothing else; see Devices and their keys.

Readings on the bus

Every reading a driver keeps is also published on a routing key, a name a message goes out under, which any queue or log bound to it gets a copy of:

Routing key What comes on it
dev.<device>.<endpoint> Each new value of the endpoint: a reading, a press, a state the device reported, a position
dev.<device> The device coming online or going offline

A message is a line of JSON that says whose it is, so it can be read without its key:

{"device":"hall-sensor","endpoint":"temperature","value":21.4,"unit":"°C","at":"2026-11-08T07:42:10.512Z"}
{"device":"porch-button","endpoint":"input-0","value":"single","event":true,"at":"2026-11-08T18:03:55.120Z"}
{"device":"ruby-phone","endpoint":"position","fix":{"lat":59.3251,"lng":18.0711,"speed":1.2},"at":"2026-11-08T12:10:04.000Z"}
{"device":"hall-plug","online":false,"at":"2026-11-08T22:15:02.004Z"}

value is a number, a word, or true and false for on and off; event marks a press; fix is a position, its speed in metres per second; at is when it was read. A reading the device replayed, as an MQTT broker does with the last one after EiN reconnects, also has "retained": true. A GPX file with thousands of positions publishes only its newest.

In a key, * stands for one part and # for any number of them, none included: dev.*.temperature is every endpoint called temperature, and dev.hall-plug.# everything the hall plug says, its coming and going included.

A queue of your own

A queue bound to keys keeps every message that comes on them until it is taken, whether or not anyone is reading. Make one in your home for what you want, such as every press of the porch button:

# in EiN's terminal
mkqueue -k 'dev.porch-button.#' ~/presses
queue-peek ~/presses     # the next ones, left in the queue
cat ~/presses            # takes them out as it prints them

Remove it with rm ~/presses when you no longer want it, or it keeps filling.

A log of your own

A log keeps what comes on its keys as entries with their times, for as long as you say:

# in EiN's terminal
mklog -k 'dev.*.temperature' -r '90 day' ~/temperatures
log-read ~/temperatures --since 1d
log-read ~/temperatures --since 30d --bucket 1d --field value --agg max

How often a reading is kept

A driver hands EiN every reading it hears, and EiN keeps and publishes it only when it is news:

  • A value the same as the endpoint's last is not kept again.
  • A number is kept at most once every minInterval seconds, the endpoint's own attribute. One that comes sooner waits for the end of the interval, and only the newest of those is kept, so the last of a burst is never lost.
  • On and off, words, positions and every press are kept as they come.

A sensor that reports its power every second therefore costs a row every ten seconds or so while it changes, and none while it holds still. The Automation module gives the endpoints it makes a minInterval from its Seconds between readings setting.
